\section{Introduction}~\label{sec:introduction}

\section{Background}~\label{sec:background}
We describe the classical Count-Min Sketch~\cite{CountMin}, Count
Sketch~\cite{CountSketch}, and Misra-Gries~\cite{MisraGries} data structures,
which form the basis upon which most other sketches are designed. These data
structures handle skew at varying levels of efficacy, making each suitable for
different contexts and workloads.

\textbf{Stream Model.}
A data stream is a sequence of item and update operation pairs~$\left\langle
(x_1,\Delta_1), (x_2,\Delta_2), \dots, \right\rangle$, where each~$x_i$ is an
item, and~$\Delta_i = \pm1$ represents whether a new~$x_i$ was inserted into
the stream ($\Delta_i=1$) or an old one was deleted ($\Delta_i=-1$). This is
known as the \emph{General Turnstile Model} of data
streams~\cite{StreamModels}. At any point, $N=\sum_i \Delta_i$ represents the
total number of items in the stream. Given a data stream, a frequency
estimation sketch provides an estimate~$\hat{f}(x)$ of the number of
times~$f(x)$ an item~$x$ has appeared in the stream.

\textbf{Count-Min Sketch (CMS).}
CMS employs an array of~$w$ counters, initialized to zero, to conduct a
frequency estimation ``experiment.'' It inserts an item~$x$ by mapping it to a
counter in the array using a hash function and incrementing the counter. CMS
estimates the frequency of item~$x$ as the value of the counter that it hashes
to. This yields an accurate estimation, as the other items in the stream likely
map to different counters, meaning that~$x$'s counter mainly tracks~$x$'s
frequency. The wider the array is, the more likely this separation of counters,
and thus, the better CMS' \emph{Accuracy} becomes. CMS handles a deletion for
item~$x$ by locating its associated counter and decrementing it.

Yet, due to hash collisions, multiple items can still hash to the same counter.
Because of this, CMS may overestimate the frequency of an item, but it never
underestimates it. To limit the overestimation error, CMS repeats the single
array experiment~$d$ times with independent arrays and hash
functions~$h_1,\dots,h_d$. \Cref{fig:sketches_overview} shows an overview of
this repeated structure. CMS now answers a query for an item~$q$ by taking the
minimum of its associated counters in all~$d$ arrays.\footnote{CMS ensures that
this reduces the estimation error by employing pairwise-independent hash
functions for~$h_1,\dots,h_d$. Doing so provides a bound on the probability of
collisions and overestimation.} Increasing the number of experiments improves
the sketch's \emph{Confidence} by lowering the probability of severe errors
without affecting the estimation accuracy.

Using~$w=\frac{e}{\epsilon}$ counters and~$d=\ln \frac{1}{\delta}$ repititions,
CMS ensures estimates have an error of at most~$\epsilon N$ with a high
confidence of~$1-\delta$, i.e., $\Pr\left( |\hat{f}(x)-f(x)| \leq \epsilon N
\right) \geq 1-\delta$ for any item~$x$. Due to its simplicity and high
accuracy, CMS is used in many applications, such as $k$-mer counting in
bioinformatics~\cite{CMSkmer,SetMinSketch}, network monitoring and traffic
estimation~\cite{SketchBasedChangeDetection,WhatsNew,TrafficManagementSurvey},
and hardware security systems~\cite{CoMeT}.

\textbf{Count Sketch (CS).}
Even though CMS is accurate, it systematically creates overestimation errors,
which may compound when the sketch is used in downstream tasks. CS addresses
this problem by providing unbiased estimates that remove such systematic
errors. As such, it is used in many applications in database systems, networks,
and machine learning, such as SQL query
optimization~\cite{Compass,QueryOptimizationSketches,ConvolutionSketch},
covariance estimation~\cite{ASCS}, feature selection~\cite{MISSION}, and
similarity
search~\cite{CSSetSimSearch,DensifyingLSHSketches,LasVegasSimSearch}. CS also
provides universality: the ability to estimate many quantities based on the
item frequencies. This makes it a general measurement tool for networks,
leading to its use in DDoS detection and many other tasks~\cite{UnivMon}.

Similarly to CMS, CS conducts an experiment using an array of~$w$ counters
coupled with a hash function~$h_i$ that maps a given item to a counter. CS
further associated a random ``update direction'' from the set~$\{-1,+1\}$ to
each item by hashing it with another hash
function~$\sigma_i$.\footnote{The~$\sigma_i$ form a 4-wise independent family
of hash functions, yielding a bound on the probability of large errors.} CS
inserts a new item~$x$ by incrementing or decrementing its counter according
to~$\sigma_i$, as shown in \Cref{fig:sketches_overview}-B. Since the update
directions are random, the expected interference on~$x$'s counter from any
other item is zero, as the positive and negative possibilities cancel out.
Thus, CS estimates~$x$'s frequency by multiplying the value of its counter
by~$\sigma_i(x)$ to revert the sign and recover the number of increments. The
zero expected interference from other items makes the average estimate equal to
the ground-truth frequency~$f(x)$, removing systematic errors. CS deletes~$x$
by subtracting~$\sigma_i(x)$ from the corresponding counter to undo the effects
of its insertion.

Hash collisions may still degrade the accuracy of this estimate in the worst
case. Having more counters in the array makes such collisions less likely,
yielding better accuracy. As with CMS, CS increases its confidence by repeating
the experiment~$d$ times. Unlike CMS, CS cannot answer a query by returning the
minimum estimate, as it may be inaccurate due to experiments suffering from
underestimations. Instead, it returns the median estimate, as shown in
\Cref{fig:sketches_overview}-B. In doing so, CS mitigates both underestimations
and overestimations simultaneously since severe hash collisions are unlikely to
occur for enough experiments to affect the median. 

Using~$w=\frac{e}{\epsilon^2}$ counters and $d=\ln \frac{1}{\delta}$
repititions, CS guarantees a high confidence of~$1-\delta$ for an estimate with
an error of at most~$\epsilon \cdot \sqrt{\sum_{y} f^2(y)}$, i.e., $\Pr\left(
|\hat{f}(x)-f(x)| \leq \epsilon \cdot \sqrt{\sum_{y} f^2(y)} \right) \geq
1-\delta$ for any item~$x$. The error term above is smaller than CMS' error
term when the frequency distribution is less skewed.

\textbf{Misra-Gries (MG).}
Under extreme skew, using dedicated counters to track the hottest items is more
beneficial than hashing them into a large array of counters as in CMS and CS.
By applying this insight, MG provides more accurate estimates under extreme
skew. As such, MG has seen widespread use as a sub-component in many sketch
designs~\cite{BatchUpdateMisraGries,AugmentedSketch,BitMatcher,BubbleSketch,HeavyGuardian,HeavyKeeper,JigsawSketch,MVSketch,SketchVisor,WavingSketch,MicroscopeSketch,PSketch}
and has been implemented in the popular Apache DataSketches
library~\cite{DataSketches}. Although certain variations of MG provide better
estimates in practice~\cite{SpaceSaving,UnbiasedSpaceSaving}, we describe the
original MG for its simplicity.

\Cref{fig:sketches_overview}-C shows an overview of MG. MG maintains an array
of slots, each storing an item identifier (shown in grey) and a dedicated
frequency counter (shown in white). This array tracks the frequencies of the
hottest items in the stream. MG inserts an incoming item~$x$ by first checking
if it has a dedicated counter in some slot. If it does, MG increments the
counter. If not, and the array has an empty slot, MG stores~$x$ in it with a
count of~$1$. If~$x$ is not in the array and the array is full, MG decrements
all counters and discards the items with zero counters. Such items are unlikely
to be among the most frequent, leading to MG emptying their slots to make room
for tracking hotter items. MG estimates~$x$'s frequency by returning the value
of its counter if it has one in the array and zero otherwise. MG only
underestimates the frequency of a counter, as it only increments the counter of
an item if it exists in the array and decrements counters otherwise. MG does
not support deletions, as decreasing counters in the array may make the
discarded items better candidates for tracking, breaking the semantics of the
array.

Having more slots in the array allows MG to track more items without discarding
them, enabling more accurate estimates. Since MG is a deterministic data
structure that does not use hashing, unlike CMS and CS, it does not suffer from
hash collisions and its estimations have full confidence.

Using an array of~$w$ slots, MG estimates the frequency of any item with an
error of at most~$\frac{N}{w}$, i.e., $|\hat{f}(x)-f(x)| \leq \frac{N}{w}$ for
any item~$x$. Since it uses dedicated counters for hot items, MG also provides
a strong ``\emph{Tail Guarantee},'' stating that the estimation error for the
$i$-th most frequent item in the stream is at
most~$\frac{N^{\text{res}(i)}}{w-i+1}$, where $N^{\text{res}(i)}$ is the total
frequency of all items not among the top~$i$. As MG also tracks the items
themselves, in contrast to CMS and CS, it is used in finding the hottest items,
items with heavy frequency changes, and much
more~\cite{BatchUpdateMisraGries,AugmentedSketch,BitMatcher,BubbleSketch,HeavyGuardian,HeavyKeeper,JigsawSketch,MVSketch,SketchVisor,WavingSketch,MicroscopeSketch,PSketch}.

\textbf{Performance.}
CMS and CS offer high performance since they read and write a single counter in
each array. In contrast, MG scans the entire array to handle an insertion or
answer a query. Typical implementations of MG use a hash table instead of the
array to speed up accesses. They also lazily decrement the counters and employ
an auxiliary heap to efficiently locate and discard items with zero counts.
However, maintaining these structures is expensive, making MG slower than CMS
and CS.

    \caption{CMS, CS, and MG provide different tradeoffs under different levels
    of skew and memory budgets. A higher characteristic exponent creates more
    skew.}
    \label{fig:classical_comparison}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Comparison.}
We compare the accuracy of CMS, CS, and MG under varying levels of data
skewness and memory budgets. We insert 100M keys drawn from the
\textsc{Zipfian} distribution into each sketch. We vary the characteristic
exponent of the distribution to control the skew of the data. We evaluate the
\emph{Average Absolute Error (AAE)} for each sketch by querying each item in
the stream, computing how far the estimate is from the ground-truth frequency,
and averaging the results.

\Cref{fig:classical_comparison} plots these results. As shown in the left
subfigure, CS beats the others when the data is less skewed, as the updates of
items with similar frequencies cancel out when their hashes collide. In
contrast, such collisions in CMS accumulate the error. MG also does not have
enough slots to provide an accurate estimation. Under moderate skew, CMS
performs the best. This is because it improves its confidence by taking the
minimum of its experiments, unlike CS, which has to be more careful about its
aggregation method. MG does not perform well here since the data is not skewed
enough. Under high skew, MG fully leverages its dedicated counters, beating
both CMS and CS.

The right plot in \Cref{fig:classical_comparison} further illustrates that,
under strict memory constraints, CS outperforms CMS. This is because of the
zero interference property of CS, making it more robust to hash collisions. In
contrast, CMS does not have enough counters to limit collisions. CMS avoids
hash collisions under a moderate memory budget and outperforms both CS and MG.
With more memory, MG tracks all heavy items and provides the most accurate
estimations.
